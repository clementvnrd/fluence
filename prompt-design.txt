(Historical. This is the brief given to a coding assistant during the app's first weeks, when it
was still a menu-bar tool. Nothing reads this file; today's design rules are in AGENTS.md.)

Turn the tool into a proper macOS app rather than something that only lives in the menu bar: an
icon in the Dock, an application menu, an ordinary resizable main window, Settings on ⌘-comma, and a
.app I can double-click and keep in Applications. Drop LSUIElement. Keep the menu bar item as a
second way in: it shows the state and stays reachable for the hotkey from any other app.

The main window should hold:
- the transcription history, searchable, with a copy button on each entry;
- a live audio level while recording;
- a start / stop control;
- the dictionary.
Settings should hold the hotkey and the choice of speech model.

DICTIONARY

I need somewhere to teach the app the words it keeps getting wrong: people's names, colleagues,
jargon, product names. I want to add, edit, delete and search entries, have them saved on disk,
and be able to edit them either in the app or as a plain text file.

Two kinds of entry:
1. a term it should recognise — e.g. Supabase, Anthropic, Vercel;
2. a pair: what it hears, and what it should write instead — e.g. "cloud code" becomes
   "Claude Code".

Use two mechanisms, because neither is enough on its own.

a) Before transcription, pass the dictionary terms to the recogniser as a hint, with whatever
   the engine supports, so it leans towards them. Keep that hint short: these models drift and
   invent words in quiet stretches when given a long context.

b) After transcription, run a correction pass over the text: whole words only, case-insensitive,
   longest trigger first. This is the part to rely on; the hint only nudges and can miss.

The correction pass must also catch a multi-word trigger that came out as one word or with a
hyphen — "Claude Code" written "CloudCode" or "Cloud-Code" — so allow optional spaces or hyphens
between the parts. It must not break real words: that rule has to leave "Cloudflare" and the
plain word "cloud" untouched, and only fire when the whole pattern matches. When a new entry
looks likely to catch everyday words, warn about it in the UI.

In the history, flag the dictations where a correction was applied and list what was replaced,
so I can tell whether the dictionary is actually helping.

Before writing any view, set up a design system: named tokens for colours, type sizes, spacing,
corner radii, borders, shadows and animation. Every view uses the tokens; no component carries
its own hard-coded values.

Visual direction (replaced since by Clement's own; kept here only as a record):
the look of 1980s tape decks and portable field recorders — think Braun, Nakamichi, the Sony
TC-D5, Marantz PMD machines. Brushed metal and matte plastic; a quiet palette of warm greys,
near-black, cream and silver; one accent only, the red of a record lamp, with amber or green for
levels. Buttons that look like they physically travel, real depth, crisp edges, visible seams
between panels. Labels small, in capitals, tightly tracked, in a neutral sans-serif; counters
and timings in segmented or monospaced digits. While recording, a needle VU meter, and level
and waveform drawn like analog instruments.

It should feel like a sober piece of equipment, not a themed skin. Not wanted: neon,
vaporwave or synthwave, purple and pink gradients, chrome lettering, glowing text, grid
horizons. Aim for the plain, workshop side of that period.

Process: show me the tokens first and wait for my go-ahead before building any view. Leave the
speech engine alone; this step is only about the interface.
